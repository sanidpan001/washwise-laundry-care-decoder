Laundry Symbols Meaning: The Complete Guide to What Do Laundry Care Symbols Mean

Every piece of clothing you own carries a small strip of fabric covered in cryptic icons — a tub with a number, a triangle, a square with a circle inside, an iron with dots. If you have ever stared at a care label and wondered what do laundry care symbols mean, you are not alone: surveys regularly find that most people ignore these symbols entirely, and ruined garments are the price. This guide explains the full laundry symbols meaning system, family by family, so you never shrink a sweater or melt a shirt again.

Care symbols are not random doodles. They follow ISO 3758, an international standard that assigns every symbol a precise meaning, so a label printed in Portugal means exactly the same thing as one printed in Bangladesh. The system uses five basic shapes, each governing one stage of garment care: the tub for washing, the triangle for bleaching, the square for drying, the iron for ironing, and the circle for professional cleaning. Learn the five shapes and their modifiers — numbers, bars, dots and crosses — and you can decode any label in the world in seconds.

The Five Families of the Laundry Symbol Chart

Think of the laundry symbol chart as five mini-languages. The tub always talks about washing: how hot the water can be and how rough the cycle may get. The triangle always talks about bleach. The square always talks about drying. The iron always talks about ironing. The circle always talks about professional dry cleaning or wet cleaning. A diagonal cross through any symbol is the universal "do not" — a crossed tub means do not wash, a crossed triangle means do not bleach, a crossed iron means do not iron. Once you internalise that grammar, even unfamiliar labels become readable.

Modifiers add the detail. A number inside the tub is the maximum wash temperature in Celsius. Bars under the tub describe cycle gentleness: no bar is a normal cycle, one bar is a gentle or permanent-press cycle with reduced agitation, and two bars is a very gentle delicates cycle with minimal spin. Dots work like a heat dial on drying and ironing symbols: one dot is low, two dots is medium, three dots is high. On the bleach triangle, diagonal stripes mean oxygen or non-chlorine bleach only. On drying squares, extra lines distinguish line drying, drip drying and flat drying, while small diagonal marks in the corner mean "dry in the shade".

Washing Symbols: The Tub

The tub is the most common symbol and the one people get wrong most often. A plain tub means machine washable on a normal cycle. A tub with "30", "40" or "60" sets the maximum water temperature — and this is a ceiling, not a target: washing at 30 when the label says 40 is always safe, but washing at 60 when it says 30 risks shrinkage and colour loss. The single most expensive mistake in home laundry is ignoring these numbers on wool, viscose and dark cottons.

Bars under the tub matter just as much as the temperature. One bar means permanent press: less agitation, a slower spin, and ideally removing clothes promptly so wrinkles do not set. Two bars mean delicates: the mildest machine setting, ideally with the garment inside a mesh laundry bag. A tub with a hand reaching into the water means hand wash only — the machine, even on a gentle cycle, is too rough. A crossed-out tub means do not wash at home at all; that garment belongs at a professional cleaner. You will also see a twisted-cloth icon with a cross, meaning do not wring — twist-drying stretches knits and damages elastic fibres, so press the water out gently or roll the garment in a towel instead.

Why temperature numbers exist

Manufacturers test fabrics at specific temperatures before printing a number. A 30°C limit usually protects dyes and shrink-prone fibres, while 60°C is reserved for sturdy whites, bedding and towels where hygiene matters — hot water kills dust mites and most bacteria. The increasingly common "wash cold" guidance is not just about fabric care; modern detergents are formulated to work at low temperatures, and cold washes dramatically cut a household's energy use. When in doubt between two numbers, always choose the lower one.

Bleaching Symbols: The Triangle

An empty triangle is a green light for any bleach, including chlorine bleach. A triangle with two diagonal stripes is the one to memorise: it means oxygen or non-chlorine bleach only, and it appears on a huge share of coloured clothing. Chlorine bleach on a striped-triangle garment will strip the colour and weaken the fibres in a single wash. A crossed triangle means no bleach of any kind — reach for a colour-safe stain remover or an oxygen-based soak instead, after a patch test.

Bleach mistakes are irreversible, which is why this family deserves respect. If a label shows the empty triangle, you still do not need bleach in every wash; it simply permits it for whitening and sanitising. And remember that "non-chlorine bleach" includes the sodium-percarbonate powders sold as colour-safe whiteners — they are gentler, but they still deserve the dosage on the packet, not a guess.

Drying Symbols: The Square

The square family is the richest on the laundry symbol chart. A circle inside the square means tumble drying is allowed, and dots inside the circle set the heat: one dot for low, two for medium, three for high, and a filled-in dot for air-fluff with no heat at all. A crossed circle-in-square means the dryer is forbidden — air dry instead. Heat is the great shrinker: cotton and wool garments that survive the wash perfectly can still come out a size smaller after one hot dryer cycle, which is why low heat is the default smart choice.

Without the circle, the square describes air drying. A curved line joining the top corners means line dry. Three short vertical lines at the top mean drip dry — hang the garment soaking wet without wringing or spinning. A single horizontal line across the middle means dry flat, the essential instruction for heavy knits and sweaters that would stretch into strange shapes on a hanger. Small diagonal lines in the square's top corner add "in the shade": direct sunlight fades colours and can make elastic brittle, so shaded drying protects darks and swimwear especially.

The dryer decisions that ruin clothes

Two dryer errors cause most damage: tumble drying something marked do-not-tumble, and using high heat on medium-heat garments. The first shrinks wool and melts heat-sensitive synthetics; the second slowly degrades elastic in underwear, activewear and swimwear until the fit gives out months early. When a load mixes heat tolerances, always dry at the lowest dot count in the load — or pull the delicates out and air dry them.

Ironing Symbols: The Iron

The iron symbol is refreshingly literal: it looks like an iron, and dots set the temperature just like your iron's own dial. One dot means a cool iron up to 110°C for delicate synthetics such as nylon and acrylic. Two dots mean a warm iron up to 150°C for wool, silk and polyester. Three dots mean a hot iron up to 200°C for cotton and linen. A plain iron with no dots allows any temperature. A crossed iron means no ironing at all — heat will scorch, melt or glaze the fabric, so hang the garment straight out of the wash instead.

Two refinements are worth knowing. An iron with its steam lines crossed out means iron dry only: steam will watermark or spot the fabric, so keep the steam function off. And some labels ask you to iron on the reverse side, which protects prints, embroidery and dark fabrics from the shiny press marks a hot soleplate can leave. A quick rule of thumb: if you are unsure, start at one dot on an inside seam and work up.

Professional Care Symbols: The Circle

The circle family speaks to dry cleaners, but you should understand it before you hand anything over. An empty circle means dry clean. Letters inside specify the solvent: A allows any solvent, P restricts the cleaner to petroleum-based solvents, and F excludes the harshest solvent, trichloroethylene. A bar under the circle means the cleaning process itself must be gentle. A crossed circle means do not dry clean — the solvents would damage the garment.

The W inside a circle is newer and increasingly common: it means professional wet cleaning, a water-based process carried out by cleaners with specialised equipment — gentler than home washing for structured garments. Bars under the W work the same way, stepping down to very gentle. A crossed W rules wet cleaning out. If your garment shows any circle symbol, resist the urge to "just hand wash it gently instead": the manufacturer has determined that home water-based cleaning is unsafe, and the cleaner's process is genuinely different from your machine.

Five Laundry Mistakes the Symbols Prevent

  • The hot wash on darks: washing a 30°C garment at 60°C fades dark colours in a handful of cycles. The number is a ceiling — respect it.
  • Chlorine bleach on colours: the striped triangle means oxygen bleach only. One cap of chlorine bleach can ruin a whole load of coloured laundry.
  • The high-heat dryer: one hot cycle can shrink a cotton shirt a full size. Match the dots, and default to low.
  • Wringing knits: twisting a wet sweater stretches it permanently. Press, do not wring, and dry flat.
  • Ironing synthetics hot: a three-dot iron on a one-dot fabric melts the fibres into a shiny, irreparable patch.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do the laundry care symbols mean?

Laundry care symbols follow the ISO 3758 standard and use five shapes: a tub for washing, a triangle for bleaching, a square for drying, an iron for ironing, and a circle for professional dry cleaning. Numbers, bars, dots and crosses modify each shape — for example, a number in the tub is the maximum wash temperature, dots set heat levels, and a cross through any symbol means "do not". Tap the symbols on your label in the decoder above for a plain-English translation.

What do the numbers inside the wash tub symbol mean?

The number inside the tub is the maximum wash temperature in degrees Celsius. A "30" means wash at 30°C or colder; "60" allows up to 60°C. It is a ceiling, not a target — washing cooler than the number is always safe, but washing hotter risks shrinkage, colour loss and fabric damage.

What do the bars under the wash symbol mean?

Bars under the tub describe how gentle the wash cycle must be. No bar means a normal cycle, one bar means a gentle or permanent-press cycle with reduced agitation and spin, and two bars mean a very gentle delicates cycle — the mildest machine treatment, ideally with the garment in a mesh laundry bag.

What do the dots on iron and tumble dryer symbols mean?

Dots are a heat dial: one dot is low heat, two dots is medium, three dots is high. On the iron, one dot means a cool iron (max 110°C), two dots a warm iron (max 150°C) and three dots a hot iron (max 200°C). In the tumble-dryer circle, the dots set the dryer heat the same way, and a filled-in dot means air-fluff with no heat.

What does the triangle symbol mean on clothes?

The triangle governs bleaching. An empty triangle means any bleach is allowed, a triangle with diagonal stripes means oxygen or non-chlorine bleach only, and a crossed triangle means do not bleach at all. Using chlorine bleach on a striped-triangle garment will strip its colour and weaken the fibres.

Can I wash something labelled "do not wash"?

No — a crossed-out tub means the garment cannot be safely washed at home by machine or by hand. Take it to a professional cleaner and follow any dry-clean or wet-clean symbol on the label. Home washing a do-not-wash garment commonly causes shrinkage, felting or structural damage that cannot be reversed.
